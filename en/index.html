Our story

Generations of one family

Our story with olives goes back generations, to a time when the land fed the family, and whatever it produced was meant for selling, sharing and trading goods.

The land that fed the family

According to our father's story, our great-grandfather and grandfather lived solely off the land — it fed the family. Alongside olives, they also grew grapevines, cherries, figs and almonds.

Cherries were sold to buyers, grapes were made into wine, figs were dried, and they also produced brandy (rakija) and olive oil.

Wine, brandy, oil and dried figs were sold, but also traded — at a time when the land was a source of food, work, trade and mutual exchange.

Our father

Born in 1939.

When he took over care of the olive groves after our grandfather's death, he continued tending the family's olives and approached it very seriously. Although he grew up around the land and olives, he believed experience alone wasn't enough — he kept learning, educating himself and improving.

"He believed the olive tree can always give its best, you just need to know how to give it what it needs."

The olive grove in Jezera

The olive grove in Jezera was our father's special love. It sits right by the sea, overlooking the Kornati islands. He planted some of the trees himself, while other, older trees were restored and continued to be tended.

"As a child I took part in planting those olive trees. For the first few years, during the summer months, after swimming I would go with my father to water the young trees, until they were strong enough to withstand the summer thirst on their own."

Tending and nature

Biodiversity first

In cultivation we use only substances allowed in organic production, and we tend the groves in a way that aims to preserve natural biodiversity.

We remove weeds mechanically, by mowing. We don't mow excessively — in spring we try to wait with mowing so the flowering meadow stays available to insects for as long as possible.

Harvest

From tree to press, the same day

In recent years the harvest has started earlier. It used to traditionally be tied to later autumn, but climate change and modern processing methods have led to an earlier start — today we most often begin around mid-October, depending on the year and the condition of the olives.

Then and now

From the old way of harvesting to today's approach

In the past, after picking, olives used to be kept in the sea until the whole harvest was finished, and only then taken for processing. That was a consequence of the conditions at the time: there was no way to store larger quantities, it wasn't possible to pick as many olives in one day as we can today, and the oil presses didn't have the capabilities they have now.

With modernization the way of harvesting changed, and with it the way of processing. Today our goal is to process the olives as soon as possible after picking, ideally the same day — olives that sit longer after picking give an oil with a different aroma and taste than one made from freshly picked olives.

We keep the tradition, but we don't do something just because that's how it used to be done.

Processing

Uljara Sv. Ivan, Vodice

The olives are processed the same day they are picked, by cold pressing.

After processing, the oil is stored in hermetically sealed stainless steel barrels, in the cellar, at a temperature of 15–20 °C.
